The History of You.

A guide to the unabridged evolution of the human lineage, from Homo sapiens back through our animal and single-celled ancestry to LUCA, the last universal common ancestor.

These are nested groups, not a procession of ancestors. “Homo sapiens ⊂ Homo” means that every human belongs to Homo. The same is true farther down the tree: we are still apes, primates, mammals, synapsids, sarcopterygians and eukaryotes.

Old structures endure. Bones once used in a jaw now carry sound through the mammalian ear. The bones of a lobe fin survive in the human arm and hand. An ancient bacterial cell survives inside us as the mitochondrion. Evolution works by inheritance, alteration and reuse.

Three clocks in deep time

Dates on an evolutionary tree can mean different things. A lineage split, the oldest known fossil and the first appearance of a trait are rarely the same date.

1 · Lineage splitThe estimated period when two populations began to diverge. Genetic clocks provide ranges, and gene flow may continue after the split begins.
2 · Oldest fossilThe oldest specimen confidently placed within a clade. It gives a minimum age: the lineage must already have existed when that organism died.
3 · Trait appearanceAn anatomical feature may appear before or after the clade name attached to it. Teeth, limbs, brains, metabolism and reproduction changed on different schedules.
Reading the dates. The ranges widen with age. Near the present, fossils and genomes give relatively dense evidence. Near LUCA, events billions of years old must be reconstructed from surviving genes, geochemistry and evolutionary models.

The last eight million years

The human form did not appear all at once. Upright walking came early. Feet, legs, pelvis, body proportions, heat control, teeth, brains, childhood and culture followed their own courses.

≈8–6 Ma split window

Human and chimpanzee lineages separate

The common ancestor was neither a chimpanzee nor a human. The earliest candidate hominins combine climbing anatomy with reduced canines and hints of upright posture. Genetic evidence also allows for continued gene flow after the first separation.

≈4.4–3.0 Ma fossils

Walking upright becomes habitual

Ardipithecus and the australopiths walked on two legs with brains still in the ape range. Pelvis, femur and knee changed while the upper body retained considerable climbing ability. By at least about 3.4 million years ago, A. afarensis had a humanlike transverse arch across the foot.

≈3.0–2.0 Ma anatomical transition

Early Homo changes diet, travel and energy use

Average brain size rose, faces and molars became smaller, tool use and food processing expanded, and life on the ground became more important. The changes did not move in lockstep, which is one reason early Homo remains difficult to classify.

≈1.9–1.5 Ma H. erectus grade

A long-legged human body takes shape

Long legs, shorter arms, large load-bearing joints, springlike feet, a narrow waist and freer shoulders made long-distance walking—and probably endurance running—far more efficient. Brain volume commonly entered the 700–1,000 cm³ range. Reduced body hair and greatly increased sweating are plausible by this period, but skin leaves no fossil record.

≈1.0–0.4 Ma multiple Homo lineages

Brains, childhood and technology expand

Several Homo populations reached brain volumes around or above 1,100 cm³. Fire, hunting, food processing and social learning increasingly changed diet, daily energy use and development. Culture itself had become part of the environment in which natural selection acted.

≈765–550 ka genomic split

The sapiens line parts from Neanderthals and Denisovans

The separation was incomplete. Later interbreeding left Neanderthal DNA in living populations outside Africa and Denisovan ancestry in several populations of Asia and Oceania. The human family tree contains branches that divided and met again.

≈315–200 ka fossils

Homo sapiens appears in Africa

Early members of our species had a relatively modern face while retaining some older cranial proportions. The rounded braincase of living humans developed later. A true chin, lighter brow ridges, a more gracile skeleton and a reorganized skull became characteristic of later H. sapiens.

A larger brain was only part of the change

Fossil averages show the broad rise: australopiths about 400–550 cm³; early Homo often 500–750 cm³; H. erectus broadly 600–1,250 cm³; later Middle Pleistocene Homo about 1,100–1,400 cm³; Neanderthals and Pleistocene H. sapiens often 1,300–1,500+ cm³. The ranges overlap. Brain organization, development, language, learning and culture cannot be reduced to cranial volume.

Bones record locomotion directly. Hair, sweat glands, vocal anatomy and many developmental changes do not fossilize, so their dates are necessarily less certain.

The full chain

Sixty-five entries lead from our species to the last universal common ancestor. Each gives an approximate date range, the wider family that joins the tree, and the principal anatomical or physiological features carried at that level.

How to read it

⊂ means “is contained in”

If A ⊂ B, every member of A also belongs to B. Moving down the list adds more distant relatives. Step 5 brings in gorillas; step 43, lampreys; step 52, choanoflagellates.

Crown and stem

A crown group contains the last common ancestor of its living members and all descendants of that ancestor. Stem groups include extinct relatives that branched off earlier. Mammaliaformes and Mammaliamorpha are therefore broader than Mammalia.

Fossils named here are cousins

Tiktaalik, Dimetrodon and Thrinaxodon are close relatives of branches in this lineage, not named links in a chain of direct ancestors. Their fossils show what bodies in those parts of the tree looked like.

Names are unstable

Clade names change as new fossils and genetic analyses alter the tree. Teleostomi has lost usefulness as acanthodians moved toward the shark side of the tree; Hominina, Podiata and Primatomorpha are also used differently by different authors. The names are labels on a changing scientific map.

Seven systems through deep time

The same history can be followed organ by organ. Bones, teeth, lungs, reproduction, senses, immunity and the cell itself each carry structures inherited from much older forms of life.

1 · Skeleton & locomotion

  1. ≈425–400 Ma lobe fins acquire the internal one-bone/two-bone architecture later reused in limbs.
  2. ≈390–360 Ma neck, wrists/ankles, digits, stronger ribs and a weight-transmitting pelvis emerge in aquatic-to-shallow-water tetrapodomorphs.
  3. ≈320 Ma amniotes become fully terrestrial; later synapsids progressively bring limbs under the body.
  4. ≈4–3 Ma habitual hominin bipedality reshapes pelvis, knee and foot.
  5. ≈2 Ma Homo evolves long legs and a spring-loaded, endurance-capable striding body.

2 · Teeth, jaw & hearing

  1. ≈450 Ma jaws arise from anterior pharyngeal-arch structures in gnathostomes.
  2. ≈300–250 Ma synapsid teeth differentiate and chewing becomes increasingly precise.
  3. ≈260–200 Ma the dentary enlarges; postdentary bones shrink; the new dentary–squamosal jaw joint takes over.
  4. ≈200–120 Ma ancestral jaw-joint bones detach and become the malleus and incus, producing the mammalian three-ossicle middle ear.
  5. ≈3 Ma–present Homo trends toward smaller molars, lighter faces and reduced chewing musculature as diet and food processing change.

3 · Breathing, circulation & heat

  1. ≈430 Ma+ bony vertebrates possess a pharyngeal gas-organ system; the sarcopterygian branch elaborates lungs.
  2. ≈370–320 Ma tetrapods shift ventilation and circulation toward sustained air breathing; amniotes refine rib ventilation and water balance.
  3. ≈280–200 Ma therapsid/cynodont posture, secondary palate and nasal anatomy support rising sustained aerobic capacity; mammalian endothermy is assembled gradually.
  4. ≈2 Ma? Homo likely intensifies eccrine sweating and reduces body hair, allowing sustained activity in heat. The timing is inferred, not fossil-observed.

4 · Reproduction & development

  1. ≈320 Ma amniotic membranes let embryos develop away from open water.
  2. ≈200 Ma+ mammary secretion, hair and prolonged parental investment are integrated into mammalian reproduction.
  3. ≈180–160 Ma therians move to live birth; eutherians and metatherians diverge.
  4. ≈100 Ma+ crown placentals elaborate prolonged gestation and maternal–fetal exchange.
  5. Homo childhood and dependency become exceptionally prolonged, moving a larger fraction of adaptation into learning after birth.

5 · Brain & senses

  1. ≈520–500 Ma vertebrate neural crest and cranial placodes build a new sensory head: inner ear, olfactory structures, cranial ganglia, facial skeleton and more.
  2. ≈70–40 Ma primates emphasize stereoscopic vision and grasping; anthropoids increase visual specialization and brain size.
  3. ≈43–31 Ma stem catarrhines acquire routine red–green trichromacy through opsin-gene duplication.
  4. ≈3 Ma–present Homo encephalization accelerates overall, but with reversals and overlap; neural reorganization and cultural inheritance prevent “brain size” from being the whole story.

6 · Immunity

  1. >600 Ma innate immune recognition is already ancient in animals.
  2. ≈520–500 Ma vertebrates possess B-like and T-like lymphocyte lineages; jawless vertebrates evolve a VLR-based adaptive system.
  3. ≈500–450 Ma jawed vertebrates acquire the RAG/V(D)J, immunoglobulin/T-cell-receptor and MHC system that humans still use.
  4. primates–humans the same ancient architecture keeps coevolving with pathogens; much of immune evolution is recent and population-specific rather than a new clade-level organ.

7 · The cell itself

  1. ≈4.2 Ga LUCA already uses ribosomes, the genetic code, ATP and sophisticated information processing.
  2. deep time Archaea evolve the host-side information-processing architecture inherited by eukaryotes.
  3. pre-LECA Asgard lineages show actin cytoskeletons, protrusions, ESCRT/ubiquitin systems and unusually strong gene duplication.
  4. ≈2 Ga an archaeal host and alphaproteobacterial endosymbiont combine; mitochondria emerge and thousands of bacterial genes are integrated into the nuclear lineage.
  5. by LECA nucleus, mitochondria, endomembranes, cytoskeleton, sex and linear chromosomes are already present.
An old body, continually altered. Human anatomy contains chordate body patterning, vertebrate sensory tissues, ancient jaws and lungs, tetrapod limbs, amniote reproduction, synapsid skull mechanics, mammalian ears and temperature control, primate hands and vision, ape shoulders, hominin bipedality and the later changes of Homo sapiens.

Where the great changes occurred

The deepest changes took millions of years and crossed the boundaries of named clades. Fossils catch them in progress: a jaw becoming an ear, a fin becoming a limb, a solitary cell acquiring the means to live as part of an animal.

1Steps 1–8: inside the apes

The names are similar—Hominina, Hominini, Homininae, Hominidae—but the branches span millions of years. When the human line separated from chimpanzees, it remained an ape lineage. The same nesting holds as gorillas, orangutans and gibbons are added farther down the tree.

Watch the ordering: Hominini (with chimps) ⊂ Homininae (with gorillas) ⊂ Hominidae (with orangutans) ⊂ Hominoidea (with gibbons).

2Steps 9–22: mammal traits arrive piecemeal

The familiar mammalian body emerged piece by piece. Across cynodonts and mammaliaforms, the secondary palate, specialized teeth, the dentary–squamosal jaw joint and the transfer of old jaw bones into the middle ear accumulated over roughly eighty million years.

The ear records the transition with unusual clarity. The articular and quadrate of the old jaw hinge became the malleus and incus. Mammalian embryos still reveal the developmental connection.

A jaw joint becomes an ear

Early jawed vertebrates carried several bones in the lower jaw and one main middle-ear ossicle. Mammals ended with a single dentary lower jaw and three ear bones. For tens of millions of years the feeding and hearing systems remained mechanically linked before the middle ear became fully detached.

3Steps 23–31: the synapsid branch

Synapsida is the mammal branch of Amniota; Sauropsida contains reptiles and birds. A single temporal opening behind the eye marks the ancestral synapsid skull. Dimetrodon, often mistaken for a dinosaur, belongs on our side of this ancient division.

The old phrase “mammal-like reptile” obscures the relationship. These animals belonged to the synapsid line, the branch that eventually produced mammals.

The slow rise of warm-blooded physiology

Posture, breathing, nasal anatomy, bone growth, insulation, metabolic rate and reproduction did not change together. Evidence for sustained high activity and temperature control accumulates across therapsids, cynodonts and mammaliaforms, long before the first crown mammals.

4Steps 32–38: fins to limbs

The pattern inside the human arm—one upper bone, two forearm bones, wrist and digits—has roots in lobe-finned fishes. A humerus, radius and ulna can already be recognized in Eusthenopteron, an animal that never left the water.

Acanthostega carried eight digits on each limb while retaining internal gills and a fin-rayed tail. Limbs and digits first evolved in water; effective life on land came later.

Limbs began in water

Digits and weight-bearing joints first appeared in animals that remained largely aquatic. Lungs were older still. The move onto land depended on modifying structures already present in shallow-water vertebrates.

5Steps 39–45: jaws, spine, notochord

Farther back lie the great vertebrate inventions: mineralized skeleton, jaws, vertebrae and the chordate body axis. The jaw arose from anterior pharyngeal arches, a developmental history still reflected in the shared genetics of jaw and ear formation.

Tunicates—not lancelets—are the closest living relatives of vertebrates. Adult sea squirts scarcely look the part, but their larvae carry a notochord and dorsal nerve cord before absorbing them during metamorphosis.

The vertebrate face is built by wandering cells

Neural crest cells migrate through the embryo to help form jaws, facial bones, teeth, pigment cells, peripheral nerves and parts of the heart and endocrine system. Cranial placodes form major sensory structures. Much of the vertebrate head begins as a developmental migration before it becomes bone and tissue.

6Steps 46–54: out of the animals

Beyond Animalia lie mostly single-celled relatives: choanoflagellates, filastereans and ichthyosporeans. Several can form temporary colonies and carry genes for adhesion and signaling that once seemed peculiar to animals.

The molecular equipment for cells to adhere, communicate and change state existed before permanent animal bodies. Animal evolution brought those behaviors under a stable developmental program.

The machinery for animals came first

Choanoflagellates, filastereans and other holozoans already possessed molecular systems for adhesion, signaling, differentiation and programmed cell death. In animals, ancient cell behaviors became coordinated across a permanent multicellular body.

7Steps 55–59: into the eukaryotic cell

At this depth the subject is no longer an animal body but the architecture of the cell: nucleus, internal membranes, cytoskeleton and mitochondria. Opisthokonts—animals, fungi and their relatives—take their name from the ancestral rear-facing flagellum, an arrangement still seen in human sperm.

LECA, the last eukaryotic common ancestor, already possessed a nucleus, mitochondria, linear chromosomes with telomeres, internal membranes and sexual reproduction. The earlier steps between an archaeal host and LECA are inferred from genomes and cell biology.

LECA was already an elaborate cell

By the time living eukaryotic lineages began to diverge, their common ancestor already had a nucleus, mitochondria, cytoskeleton, endomembranes, linear chromosomes, telomeres and meiotic sex. The origin of these systems lies deeper on the eukaryotic stem.

8Steps 60–65: archaea and LUCA

In the two-domain tree, eukaryotes arise from within Archaea. Asgard archaea occupy the closest known prokaryotic neighborhood to that origin, placing the deepest division in this history between the bacterial and archaeal branches descending from LUCA.

A 2024 reconstruction places LUCA at roughly 4.2 billion years ago, only a few hundred million years after Earth formed. The study inferred a genome of about 2.5 megabases and roughly 2,600 proteins, an anaerobic acetogenic metabolism and an ecosystem already containing other lineages.

Asgard archaea and the eukaryotic origin

Phylogenomic work continues to place Asgard archaea close to the origin of eukaryotes. Their genomes show extensive horizontal gene transfer as well as unusually high rates of gene duplication, helping explain their large molecular repertoire. The exact branch from which eukaryotes arose remains unsettled.

The mitochondrial graft

At the origin of eukaryotes, a simple branching tree is no longer enough. The cell that eventually gave rise to animals joined two ancient lineages.

The human mitochondrial genome is a bacterial remnant only 16,569 base pairs long. Most genes once carried by the endosymbiont moved into the nucleus during eukaryotic evolution, leaving bacterial and archaeal histories intertwined within the same cell.

The main list follows the host lineage through the nuclear genome into Archaea. The mitochondrial branch enters from Alphaproteobacteria and therefore appears beside, rather than within, the nested chain.

Here the tree becomes a network

The nuclear lineage points into Archaea; the mitochondrion points into Bacteria. Gene transfer later moved much of the mitochondrial genome into the nucleus. Every human cell carries the record of that ancient merger.

The Asgard question

Several placements still have serious support, and no single topology has ended the debate:

  • Hodarchaeales-sister. Eme and colleagues (Nature, 2023) place eukaryotes as the sister lineage of Hodarchaeales, an order within Heimdallarchaeia. This is what steps 60–61 of the list encode.
  • Outside Heimdallarchaeia. Zhang and colleagues (Nature, 2025) recover eukaryotes branching deeper in the Asgard tree, outside Heimdallarchaeia altogether. If that holds, step 61 drops out.
  • Three domains. Forterre (2025) argues that the eukaryote–Asgard signal reflects extensive lateral gene transfer between proto-eukaryotes and Heimdallarchaeia rather than direct descent, and that the eukaryotes-inside-Archaea result appears in a minority of individual marker trees. Under this reading the two-domain tree is an artefact and Eukaryota remains a separate domain.

Recent phylogenomic analyses continue to place Asgard archaea as the closest known prokaryotic relatives of eukaryotes, while disagreeing over the exact point of attachment. Some recover eukaryotes within or beside Heimdallarchaeia; others place the origin deeper in Asgard. The page therefore marks this part of the tree as unresolved.

A shorter backbone

The full chain is meant for reference. For memory, these larger landmarks give the shape of the lineage; the finer clades can be fitted between them.

Homo sapiens → Hominoidea → Primates → Placentalia → Mammalia → Cynodontia → Synapsida → Amniota → Tetrapoda → Sarcopterygii → Osteichthyes → Gnathostomata → Vertebrata → Chordata → Bilateria → Animalia → Choanozoa → Holozoa → Opisthokonta → Eukaryota → Asgard archaea → Archaea → LUCA

Where the evidence is strongest

High confidence: the broad nesting of humans within mammals, tetrapods, vertebrates, animals and eukaryotes; major homologies such as limb bones, jaw-to-ear bones and chordate development; mammalian lactation; and the bacterial origin of mitochondria. Moderate confidence: many molecular split ranges and reconstructions of ancestral physiology. Active debate: several deep dates and named clades, the animal root, parts of deuterostome phylogeny and the exact Asgard branch closest to eukaryotes.

The record is sharp in some places and hazy in others. Fossils, embryos, genomes and living organisms overlap to give a remarkably detailed history, while the oldest branches remain open to revision.
